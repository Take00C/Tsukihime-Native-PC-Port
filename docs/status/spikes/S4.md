# Spike S4: the largest picture through a C# MZX0 + MZP prototype, parallel tiles, sliced upload

**Verdict: PASS** (fallback not triggered), with conditions on the upload settings.

- **Decode.** The largest picture (allpac1 `IMG1481.MZP`, 11,220 x 7,650 shown, 343,332,000 bytes
  RGBA) is read and decoded in 32 ms with 16 workers (38 ms with an uncached disk read). Single
  threaded it takes 172 ms. The threshold is 500 ms on the workers.
- **Upload.** With the settings of section 5.3, 0 of 5,103 load frames took more than 16.7 ms,
  over 170 runs; with the bounded staging pool added, 0 of 2,423 over 90 runs. The worst frame was
  8.95 ms, at a 6.94 ms vsync. The threshold is that no presented frame takes more than 16.7 ms
  during the upload.
- **Settings that fail the frame threshold** were measured too: a PBO ring, allocating every tile
  texture at once, time-capped batches (4 ms of CPU, up to 64 tiles), and 14 workers on a busy
  machine (section 5.2).
- **Correctness.** The C# RGBA equals the Python oracle (SHA-1) for 154 of 154 pictures across
  all four pixel formats. Every upload run read its textures back from the GPU, and the read-back
  equals the oracle in 205 of 205 runs.

Owner: stream D. Every number below comes from runs on 2026-10-25 between 21:15 and 21:28 UTC
(222 result files). They ran at commit `3851949`, plus the uncommitted tree (dirty: the spike is
not committed). The exploratory runs and the earlier matrix of section 5.2 ran before that, at
`cd94425` (dirty). Release build, no debugger attached.

The machine was shared with other agents' jobs during the runs, so every upload run records the
machine-wide CPU load (`GetSystemTimes`). That load was 11-100 % during the load phase. Section 7
covers what this means.

## 5. Upload: sliced, while presenting

`S4.BigPicture upload`. A 1280 x 720 window, GL 3.3 core (`3.3.0 Core Profile Context
26.10.1.261008`, AMD Radeon RX 9070 XT), vsync on (swap interval 1). The monitor runs at 144 Hz,
and the measured vsync is 6.93-6.95 ms p50.

Each run has 120 warm-up frames, of which the first 60 are excluded from the statistics. At frame
120 the decode workers start, and the uploads begin as soon as tiles are ready. Then come 60 tail
frames. "Load frames" are the frames from the decode start to the frame that uploads the last
tile. "Frame" is the time between two `SDL_GL_SwapWindow` returns.

"Upload CPU" is the time spent in the per-frame upload block (`glTexImage2D` / `glTexSubImage2D`
from client memory, plus returning the buffer). "GPU" is `GL_TIME_ELAPSED` around that block. It
stays near zero because the copies are asynchronous DMA, so it does not measure the transfer.

Setting names: `tN` = at most N tiles per frame; `m16` = at most 16 MiB per frame (at least one
tile); `b4ms` = keep uploading until 4 ms of CPU time; `wN` = N decode workers at below-normal
priority; `dec` = decode-only control with no upload; `pool` = all tile textures created and
first-touched during the warm-up (a texture cache that already holds free textures); `s64` = a
bounded 64 MiB staging pool, pre-touched, that workers wait on; `pre` = all textures allocated
in the first load frame; `pbo` = a 64-PBO ring (orphan, map, memcpy, unmap, upload from PBO).

Where the failing frames' time goes (`draw_ms` and `swap_ms` are recorded per frame):

- **`pre`.** The frame after the 330 `glTexImage2D(NULL)` calls stalls 24-37 ms inside
  `SwapWindow`. The calls themselves take 1.5-1.8 ms. The driver commits the texture memory at the
  flush.
- **`pbo`.** Frames with 16 PBO uploads spend 11-16 ms in `SwapWindow`, against about 5 ms with
  direct uploads. On this driver a PBO ring is slower than uploading from client memory.
- **`b4ms`.** A 4 ms CPU cap admits 21-29 tiles (MiB) in a frame. The driver then spends
  25-38 ms in `SwapWindow`. A CPU-time cap does not bound the work the driver defers to the
  flush, so the cap has to be in bytes.
- **`t4_w14`.** The 66.8 ms and 34.5 ms frames spend 4.5 / 0.6 / 0.1 ms in upload / draw /
  swap. The rest is outside the GL calls: the main thread was not running. The machine was 99 %
  and 61 % busy in those runs, with 14 decode workers plus the other jobs.

### 5.3 Upload rules that the measurements support

1. **Decode workers: at most the physical core count (8 here), below-normal priority, on
   dedicated threads.** With 14 workers and 4 tiles per frame, a busy machine produced frames of
   34.5 and 66.8 ms. With 8 workers there were 0 such frames. The decode is still only 26-47 ms
   for the largest pictures (section 4).
2. **Cap each frame's slice in bytes, at about 16 MiB, and always upload at least one tile.**
   32 MiB per frame still had no frame over 16.7 ms, but it reached 11.3 ms, with 6.6 ms of upload
   CPU per frame at the median and 10.9 ms at most. That leaves too little for the frame's own work.
3. **Upload straight from client memory** (`glTexImage2D` for a new texture, `glTexSubImage2D` for
   a recycled one). Do not use a PBO ring on this driver.
4. **Never allocate many textures in one frame.** Allocate lazily when a tile is uploaded, or
   recycle textures from the LRU cache. Pre-creating them over the warm-up frames (`pool`) works
   too.
5. **Use a bounded staging pool of about 64 MiB of pre-touched tile buffers that the workers wait
   on.** The load time is unchanged (146 ms for IMG1481, 272 ms for the heavy scene). The peak
   working set falls from 359-603 MB to 241-279 MB, and native buffers stay at 67 MiB.

## 6. Scene-load cost: backdrop + 3 sprites

The scene sets come from script usage, not from a guess. `s4_oracle.py` walks every STCH/SCH2
of the 549 scripts with `tsuki.script` (59,164 picture statements, 10,919 distinct pictures). It
splits the catalog pictures into two pools:

- backdrops: 5,590 catalog records without a character id and at least 1920 x 1080, used by
  46,290 statements;
- sprites: 3,982 records with a character id, used by 9,062 statements; each sprite is its base
  plus face layers.

It draws 24 scenes (seed 4), each one backdrop plus 3 sprites, weighted by usage. A base shared by
two sprites is decoded once, as the texture cache would do. The "heavy" scene is the largest

| heavy scene | 622 MiB | 40 | 271.6 | 667 | 7.63 |

Extrapolation for the runtime: a typical scene change is about 3 ms of read and 6-14 ms of decode.
The upload is the long part: 8-11 frames at 16 MiB per frame. The scene is fully resident about
50-70 ms after the decode starts at 144 Hz, or about 0.13-0.18 s at 60 Hz. The largest picture
and the heavy scene need 22 / 40 frames, which is 0.37 / 0.67 s at 60 Hz.

None of this blocks a frame. The constraint is only lead time: the prefetcher (engine 5.4) must
queue a scene 8-40 frames before its first draw. For comparison, the PS4 prefetch byte budget of
12,000,000 bytes covers the compressed size of a p90 typical scene (14.3 MB) only partly, and
IMG1481 (47.3 MB) not at all. The port's prefetcher should therefore be bounded in decoded bytes
and frames, not in the PS4 budget.

GPU memory: a typical scene holds 100-150 MiB of tile textures, IMG1481 330 MiB and the heavy
scene 622 MiB. The 3 GB default texture cache (ARCHITECTURE 4.3) holds about 20-30 typical
scenes or about 4 heavy ones.

## 7. Verdict against the thresholds, and caveats

| threshold (ROADMAP M0) | measured | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| decode <= 500 ms on workers | IMG1481 read + decode: 32.0 ms (16 workers), 37.9 ms with an uncached read, 43.0 ms cold. Worst picture for MZX0 (IMG1481_20_00): 55.2 ms. With 8 workers: 29.8 / 63.8 ms | **PASS** |
| no presented frame > 16.7 ms during the upload | 0 of 5,103 load frames over 170 runs with the 5.3 rules, worst 8.95 ms; 0 of 2,423 over 90 runs with the staging pool. The rules matter: the variants that break them fail (5.2) | **PASS** |
| C# RGBA equals the Python oracle | 154 / 154 pictures; GPU read-back equal in 205 / 205 upload runs | **PASS** |

Caveats:

- **Shared machine.** Other jobs ran during the runs (machine-wide busy 11-100 % during the load
  phases). This mostly makes the frame results conservative. It also makes the residual hitch
  risk hard to pin down: the earlier build had 4 of 805 frames over 16.7 ms at `t16_w8` without
  a staging pool. The T7 soak test (ARCHITECTURE 10) should measure the hitch rate over hours on
  an otherwise idle machine.
- **144 Hz monitor.** The display ran at 144 Hz, so a frame budget of 6.94 ms was tested, which is
  stricter than 16.7 ms. The 60 Hz times in section 6 are computed from the measured frame
  counts, not measured.
- **One GPU and driver.** Results are for this card and driver (AMD RX 9070 XT, GL driver
  26.10.1). The PBO finding and the `pre` stall are driver behaviour. GLES and other vendors were
  not tested.
- **Timer queries.** With the 5.3 settings, `GL_TIME_ELAPSED` around the uploads reads at most
  0.05-0.1 ms per frame into recycled textures and 1.5-3.3 ms when new textures are allocated. The
  copies themselves are asynchronous DMA. Use frame time and swap time as the metric, not timer
  queries.

## 9. Notes for the real implementation

- **`Tsuki.Data` (stream A/C).** The decoder shape that met every target is: MZX0 with 8-byte
  copies and the 128-byte overrun slack, then a per-tile conversion into a stored-tile RGBA buffer,
  border included. That buffer is the upload source, and no displayed bitmap is assembled at
  runtime. `samples/spikes/s4/oracle.json` holds 154 oracle SHA-1s across all four formats, which
  can serve as golden values for `Tsuki.Tests.Data`. They have to be regenerated by the golden
  exporter, not copied, because `samples/golden/` is generator-owned.
- **`Tsuki.Render` (stream D).** One texture per stored tile (512 x 512 = 1 MiB). Apply the five
  rules of 5.3 in the upload scheduler: about 16 MiB per frame, at least one tile; no PBO; lazy or
  recycled textures; a bounded staging pool; workers <= physical cores at below-normal priority.
- **Prefetch.** Typical scenes need 8-11 frames of lead time and giant pictures 22-40 frames
  (section 6). The ROADMAP fallbacks (an earlier prefetch distance, the opt-in decoded cache) are
  not needed on this machine.
